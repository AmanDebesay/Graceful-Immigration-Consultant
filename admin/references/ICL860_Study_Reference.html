Introduction to Refugee Law

Who is a refugee? Core definitions & the framework of protection
📖

Core Question: Who Is a Refugee?

▼

A refugee is someone who cannot or will not return to their country because of a well-founded fear of persecution on specific grounds, or because they face serious harm.

The Three Case Studies (Who qualifies?)
Iman (Syria)
  • Fled civil war / indiscriminate bombings
  • Assad regime now overthrown
  • Issue: Indiscriminate harm ≠ persecution on grounds; changed country conditions
Jose (Pacific Island)
  • Climate change = island uninhabitable
  • Disability makes temp shelter impossible
  • Issue: Climate displacement not a Refugee Convention ground; govt provided shelter
Adeel (Convention Refugee with crimes)
  • Recognized as a Convention refugee
  • Subsequently joined a criminal gang; committed serious crimes
  • Issue: Danger to the public in Canada → possible exception to non-refoulement under s.115(2)
⚖️

Non-Refoulement — IRPA s.115

▼
"A protected person shall not be removed from Canada to a country where they would be at risk of persecution for reasons of race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group or political opinion or at risk of torture or cruel and unusual treatment or punishment." IRPA s.115(1)
Exceptions (s.115(2)) — Applies if person is:
Exception (a)
  • Inadmissible on grounds of serious criminality
  • AND constitutes a danger to the public in Canada
Exception (b)
  • Inadmissible on grounds of security, human rights violations, or organized criminality
  • AND should not remain in Canada based on acts committed or danger to security
⚠️ Even with exceptions, removal to torture is generally prohibited under international law (CAT).

The Refugee Journey — Step by Step

▼
Claim Initiated
POE or IRCC Portal → Eligibility
IRCC/CBSA → Referred to RPD
+ Conditional Departure Order → RPD Hearing
Decision → RAD Appeal
(if eligible) → Federal Court
Judicial Review → CBSA Enforcement
Removal Order → PRRA
s.112 (if still in Canada)
Two entry points: Port of Entry (POE) — claim made at the border; Inland — claim made after entry into Canada via IRCC portal.
📜

RPD Rules vs RAD Rules — Key Differences

▼
RPD — First Instance Hearing
  • Basis of Claim (BOC) form required
  • Inquisitorial model (member leads)
  • Oral hearing standard
  • Claimant testifies; witnesses possible
  • Documents submitted in advance
RAD — Appeal of RPD Decision
  • Generally paper-based (no oral hearing by default)
  • Review RPD errors of law/fact
  • New evidence very restricted (s.110(4))
  • 15 days to file Notice of Appeal
  • 45 days to perfect (file full record)
Common to Both
Interpreter available Counsel permitted Procedural fairness obligations Applications for accommodation

Module 3

Inclusion — Part 1

Section 96: The Convention Refugee definition — subjective fear, objective basis, persecution
📝

The s.96 Definition — Broken Down

▼
  • Person is outside their country of nationality (or former habitual residence)
  • Has a well-founded fear of persecution
  • Fear is linked to a Convention ground (nexus)
  • Is unable or unwilling to avail themselves of state protection
💡 The claimant only needs to satisfy s.96 OR s.97 — not both. But the RPD/RAD must assess both.
🧠

Well-Founded Fear — Subjective + Objective

▼
Subjective Fear
  • Claimant genuinely fears persecution
  • Undermined by:
  • Delay in leaving country
  • Delay in claiming in Canada
  • Failure to claim in a third country
  • Re-availment of country's protection
Objective Basis
  • Fear must be objectively reasonable
  • Standard: "reasonable chance" / "serious possibility" / "good grounds"
  • Assessed via country conditions evidence (NDP, reports)
  • Mixed evidence must be weighed
🔥

What Counts as "Persecution"?

▼
Serious harm Fundamental violation of human rights State accountability required

Persecution = more than mere discrimination. Can be:

  • Repetitive and persistent acts (cumulative discrimination)
  • One-off but severe (e.g., torture, rape)
  • NOT: ordinary criminal law enforcement, general poverty, or natural disaster
⚠️ Discrimination alone ≠ persecution unless it rises to a fundamental human rights violation.
📋

Onus, Identity & Credibility

▼
Onus
  • Claimant bears the burden of proof
  • Standard: balance of probabilities
  • Not "beyond reasonable doubt"
Starting Point
  • Claimant's sworn testimony = presumed true
  • Maldonado: Swearing to truth creates presumption of credibility
  • RPD must give reasons to doubt
👤

Case Study: Farah Aziz — Applying s.96

▼

The Farah Aziz exercise applies the full s.96 framework to a concrete BOC narrative:

  • Is she outside her country of nationality?
  • Does she have a subjective fear? (behaviour analysis)
  • Is the fear objectively well-founded? (country conditions)
  • Is what she fears "persecution"?
  • Is there a nexus to a Convention ground?
  • Is state protection available?
  • Is there an IFA?
Module 4

Inclusion — Part 2

Nexus, State Protection, IFA, and Section 97 Complementary Protection
🔗

Nexus — Convention Grounds (s.96)

▼

The persecution must be for reasons of one or more of these 5 grounds:

Race Religion Nationality Political Opinion Membership in a Particular Social Group (PSG)
💡 "Particular Social Group" is the most litigated ground — requires an immutable characteristic and social recognition.

In Biman Sikdar's case: targeted for involvement with the Bangladesh National Party (BNP) → nexus to political opinion.

🛡️

State Protection — The Framework

▼
"Only in situations in which state protection might reasonably have been forthcoming will the claimant's failure to approach the state for protection defeat his claim." Canada (Attorney General) v. Ward [1993] 2 SCR 689
The Framework
  • There is a presumption that states can protect their nationals
  • Claimant must rebut this presumption with clear and convincing evidence
  • More democratic the country → stronger the presumption
  • Claimant need NOT approach state if protection would not reasonably have been forthcoming
Evidentiary Burden
To Rebut Presumption
  • National documentation package (NDP)
  • Personal evidence of failed attempts
  • Expert evidence on country conditions
  • Media / human rights reports
Felippe Eli Exercise
  • Practice questioning a client about state protection
  • Did they approach authorities?
  • What happened when they did?
  • Would it be reasonable to expect protection?
🗺️

Internal Flight Alternative (IFA)

▼

Even if there's persecution in one part of the country, the claim fails if there's a viable IFA elsewhere in the country.

Two-Pronged Test
Prong 1 — No Serious Risk
  • Claimant would NOT face persecution in the IFA location
  • Agent of harm cannot reach the person there
Prong 2 — Reasonably Viable
  • It is reasonable for the person to relocate there
  • Consider: gender, age, disability, language, economic access
  • Not unduly harsh
Colombia IFA Scenario (Key Arguments)
Client from Bogotá being asked to relocate to Tunja. AGC (paramilitary group) has national reach. Domestic violence widespread. Government laws improving but implementation criticized.

Arguments against IFA: AGC nationwide operations; domestic violence support inadequate in smaller cities; client has no contacts/support in Tunja.
🆚

Section 97 — Person in Need of Protection

▼

s.97 is complementary to s.96 — it protects people who don't fit the Convention refugee definition but still face serious harm.

s.96 Convention Refugee
  • Outside country of nationality
  • Subjective + objective fear
  • Persecution
  • Nexus to Convention ground
  • State protection unavailable
  • No viable IFA
s.97 Person in Need of Protection
  • In Canada
  • Risk of: torture, risk to life, cruel/unusual treatment
  • Risk must be personal (not generalized)
  • Not inherent to lawful sanction
  • Not inability to provide medical care
  • State protection unavailable; no IFA
⚠️ Key difference: s.97 does NOT require a nexus to a Convention ground, but the risk must be personal and individualized, not shared by all citizens generally.
Module 5

Exclusion

Articles 1E and 1F — When protection is denied despite facing harm
🚫

Exclusion — General Principles

▼

Exclusion means the person may face real risk, but is denied refugee status because of their own conduct or status. The exclusion clauses are in the Refugee Convention and incorporated into Canadian law.

Art. 1E — Rights equivalent to nationality elsewhere Art. 1F(a) — War crimes / Crimes against humanity Art. 1F(b) — Serious non-political crimes Art. 1F(c) — Acts against UN principles
⚠️ Exclusion is assessed on "serious reasons for considering" — a lower standard than balance of probabilities.
🌍

Article 1E — Status in Another Country

▼
"This Convention shall not apply to a person who is recognized by the competent authorities of the country in which he has taken residence as having the rights and obligations which are attached to the possession of the nationality of that country." Refugee Convention Art. 1E
Zeng Framework (FCA 2010) — Three Scenarios
If the claimant had the rights when they left that country
  • → Excluded (unless rights subsequently lost due to state action)
If the claimant does NOT currently have such rights
  • → Not excluded (but RPD may consider why rights were lost)
Voluntarily given up rights
  • → Generally excluded
⚔️

Article 1F(a) — War Crimes & Crimes Against Humanity

▼
Crimes Against Peace
  • Planning, preparing, or waging a war of aggression
War Crimes
  • Serious violations of laws and customs of war
Crimes Against Humanity
  • Murder, enslavement, torture, etc.
  • In peace or war
  • Committed in widespread/systematic fashion
  • Defined in the Rome Statute
Defence: Duress
  • Threat of imminent harm (will be carried out)
  • No safe avenue of escape
  • Close temporal connection
  • Harm inflicted proportionate to harm threatened
Complicity Standard — Ezokola Test
Ezokola v. Canada [2013] SCC 40
"Serious reasons for considering that an individual has voluntarily made a significant and knowing contribution to a group's crime or criminal purpose."
Syrian Air Force Case Study
Colonel who maintained weapon systems used by pilots bombing protesters. Key question: Did he make a significant and knowing contribution? Review of targeting videos after attacks → likely yes.
🔫

Article 1F(b) — Serious Non-Political Crimes

▼
  • Crime committed outside Canada before entering Canada
  • Must be a "serious" crime (consider: nature, sentence, circumstances, mitigating factors)
Is it "Non-Political"?

A crime is political if there is:

  • A political disturbance (struggle to modify/abolish a government or policy)
  • A rational nexus between the crime and the political objective
Juan's Case Study
Convicted in the US for armed motor vehicle theft ($5,000 car) at age 22 while addicted to drugs. Served 7 years. Now facing refugee exclusion.

Factors weighing against exclusion: Drug addiction (mitigating), single offence, rehabilitation, no other criminal history, time elapsed.
Factors for exclusion: Knife used (violence), serious sentence, committed abroad.
🌐

Article 1F(c) — Acts Against UN Principles

▼

Applies to acts contrary to the purposes and principles of the UN Charter. Very rarely invoked. Generally aimed at:

Terrorism Financing of terrorism Systematic human rights violations by state actors

Trauma-Informed Service & Credibility

Supporting vulnerable clients and understanding how credibility is assessed
💛

Principles of Trauma-Informed Legal Practice

▼
Core Characteristics
  • Safety — physical and emotional
  • Trustworthiness and transparency
  • Peer support and collaboration
  • Empowerment and choice
  • Cultural, historical, gender sensitivity
In Practice
  • Use grounding techniques when client dissociates
  • Explain every step of the process
  • Allow client to set pace
  • Avoid re-traumatizing through excessive detail
  • Separate emotion from legal analysis when writing
"Grounding techniques help the person step out of the movie theater into the daylight." HHS — Trauma-Informed Care
📋

IRB Guideline 8 — Vulnerable Persons

▼

Guideline 8 provides procedural accommodations for vulnerable persons at the IRB.

Who May Be Designated Vulnerable?
Minors (under 18) Survivors of trauma or torture People with mental health conditions Survivors of sexual/gender-based violence
Accommodations Available
Designated Representative Testimony via videoconference Breaks during hearing Shortened sessions Support person present Private hearing room
Designated Representative (s.20 RPD Rules)
A designated representative is required when:
  • Claimant is under 18
  • Claimant is unable to appreciate the nature of proceedings
Counsel must notify the RPD without delay if they believe a DR should be appointed.
⚖️

Credibility — The Framework

▼
Maldonado — Starting Presumption
"When an applicant swears to the truth of certain allegations, this creates a presumption that those allegations are true unless there be reason to doubt their truthfulness."
What Can Rebut the Presumption?
Common Negative Findings
  • BOC omissions (key facts not mentioned)
  • Inconsistencies (internal or with documents)
  • Absence of corroborating evidence
  • Problems with documents submitted
  • Implausibility findings
Procedural Fairness Required
  • Claimant must be given opportunity to explain
  • RPD cannot make negative findings without putting concerns to claimant first
  • Rahal v. Canada — stern warning against prejudging
❌ Unreasonable inferences the RPD should NOT make:
  • Using travel on false documents to impugn overall credibility
  • Assuming all documents from certain countries are fake
  • Cultural stereotypes about how victims "should" behave
🏳️‍🌈

Gender & SOGIESC Guidelines

▼
Guideline 4 — Gender Considerations

Recognizes that women and gender-diverse people may face persecution differently and that conventional credibility assumptions may not apply.

SOGIESC — Myths the IRB Must AVOID
❌ Do NOT assume:
  • SOGIESC individuals have feminized/masculinized appearances
  • Would not voluntarily enter heterosexual marriage
  • Would actively participate in LGBTIQ2 culture in Canada
  • Are promiscuous or sexually active
✅ Best practices:
  • Allow claimant to tell their own story
  • Avoid intrusive questioning about sexual practices
  • Recognize that coming out is a process, not an event

Proceedings Before the IRB

RPD hearings, RAD appeals, timelines, writing arguments, and the CREAC method
🏛️

RPD Hearing — What to Expect

▼
1. Opening — Member introduces hearing, identifies counsel, confirms interpreter
↓
2. Preliminary applications (e.g., accommodations, late documents)
↓
3. Member questions claimant (inquisitorial model — member leads)
↓
4. Minister's counsel cross-examines (if intervening)
↓
5. Claimant's counsel may question (clarifying, not repeating)
↓
6. Submissions by parties
↓
7. Decision (oral or reserved)
📅

RAD Appeals — Timelines & Key Rules

▼
Key Deadlines
  • 15 days — Notice of Appeal after RPD written reasons
  • 45 days — Perfect the record (Appellant's Record) after written reasons
  • Regulations say 30 days; RAD practice notice allows 45
New Evidence (s.110(4) IRPA)
  • Very restricted on appeal
  • Must have arisen after RPD hearing, OR
  • Was not reasonably available, OR
  • Claimant could not reasonably have been expected to present it
Oral Hearing at RAD
An oral hearing at the RAD is exceptional — only when new evidence raises a credibility issue that is central to the decision.
Standard of Review
Correctness
  • Questions of law — RAD decides independently
Reasonableness
  • Questions of fact/mixed — defer somewhat to RPD but RAD can intervene
📝

RAD Record — What Goes In

▼
  • Notice of decision and written reasons (optional per practice notice)
  • Transcript or CD timestamp reference
  • Any evidence RPD refused to accept
  • Written statement (re: new evidence, oral hearing request, interpreter needs)
  • New evidence (if admissible)
  • Case law (or list of cases)
  • Memorandum (legal argument)
Memorandum Must Contain (RAD Rule 3(g))
  • Errors alleged at the RPD
  • Where those errors appear (paragraph numbers, transcript)
  • How new evidence meets s.110(4)
  • The decision sought from the RAD
  • Why an oral hearing should be held (if requested)
✍️

CREAC — Legal Argument Framework

▼
CONCLUSION — State the issue revealing your conclusion upfront
RULE — Set out the governing law or legal principles
EXPLANATION — Explain the rule / what the cases say
APPLICATION — Apply the law to your specific facts
CONCLUSION — State the outcome of applying law to facts
Example: Francisco Ramírez López (State Protection Error)
C:
The RPD erred by finding the appellant failed to rebut the presumption of state protection without analyzing whether it would have been forthcoming.

R: Ward — failure to seek state protection defeats a claim only if it "might reasonably have been forthcoming."

E: RPD must analyze evidence before faulting claimant.

A: CJNG is Mexico's most powerful cartel; police underfunded and corrupt; no analysis conducted.

C: Reviewable error — decision must be quashed. (See: Perez Mendoza 2010 FC 119)
⚠️

Common RPD Errors to Argue on Appeal

▼
Errors of Law
  • Wrong legal test applied
  • Ignoring relevant legal principle
  • Incorrect statutory interpretation
  • No analysis of whether state protection would be forthcoming before faulting claimant
Errors of Fact
  • Ignoring or mischaracterizing evidence
  • Unreasonable credibility findings
  • Implausibility findings without basis
  • Failing to consider cumulative evidence
Practical Tips
  • Always cite paragraph numbers from RPD decision
  • Start with your strongest argument
  • Use point-first (CREAC) writing throughout
  • Number your paragraphs
  • Cite CanLII references with pinpoint cites
  • Double-check all deadlines

Resettlement & Rights of Refugees

What happens after protection: cessation, vacation, rights, and Bill C-2 changes
🏠

Classes of Refugees for Resettlement

▼
Government-Assisted Refugees (GAR)
  • Sponsored by the Canadian government
  • UNHCR usually identifies candidates
Privately Sponsored Refugees (PSR)
  • Sponsored by private groups or designated organizations
  • 5-year sponsorship commitment
Blended Visa Office Referred (BVOR)
  • Combination: government + private sponsors share costs
General Eligibility Requirements
  • Outside Canada
  • Not a threat to Canadian security
  • Refugee need
  • Ability to establish in Canada
🔚

Cessation — Losing Protected Person Status

▼

Cessation ends refugee protection when the basis for it no longer exists.

Three Requirements for Cessation
1. Voluntariness
  • The refugee's action must be voluntary
2. Intention
  • Must intend to re-avail of country's protection
3. Re-availment
  • Must actually obtain such protection
Camayo Factors — Rebutting Presumption of Reavailment
MCI v. Camayo, 2022 FCA 50
Obtaining a passport from country of origin + travelling there creates a presumption of reavailment. To rebut it, consider:
  • State of the claimant's knowledge about cessation
  • Personal attributes (age, education, sophistication)
  • Identity of the agent of persecution
  • Whether the passport was obtained voluntarily
  • Whether the passport was actually used for travel
  • Purpose of travel (compelling reason = serious illness/death of family member)
  • Frequency and duration of travel
  • Activities while in the country
  • Precautionary measures taken
  • Whether actions indicate loss of subjective fear
Wei's Case Study
Falun Gong practitioner; refugee since 2010. Renewed Chinese passport in 2011. Traveled to Hong Kong (2013) to see ill mother, and to China (2014) for her funeral. Attended a high school reunion in Hong Kong.

For cessation (Minister): Obtained passport; voluntarily traveled; attended social events; dealt with Chinese officials without incident.
Against cessation (Refugee): Compelling reason (dying/deceased parent); traveled to Hong Kong, not China proper for first trip; duty as only child; no loss of subjective fear otherwise shown.
📛

Vacation — Protected Status Obtained Through Fraud

▼

Vacation removes refugee protection where it was obtained through:

Misrepresentation Withholding of material facts Fraud
⚠️ Vacation is different from cessation — it looks backward (how protection was obtained) rather than forward (changed circumstances).
✅

Rights of Refugee Claimants & Protected Persons

▼
While Claim is Pending
  • Right to counsel
  • Work permit eligible
  • Access to health care (IFHP)
  • Cannot be removed while claim is pending
Once Protected Person Status Granted
  • Apply for Permanent Residence
  • Can sponsor certain family members
  • Right to travel document (not passport)
  • Cannot obtain passport from persecutor country (risk of cessation)

Bill C-2 — Proposed Legislative Changes

▼
New Ineligibility Provisions
  • One-year bar: Refugee claim ineligible if more than 1 year has passed since claimant first arrived in Canada (after June 24, 2020)
  • Irregular crossers from US: Claim ineligible if person crossed irregularly from the US and makes a claim more than 14 days after crossing
Changes to In-Canada Asylum System
  • No more procedural distinction between inland and POE claims
  • Claim sent to RPD for abandonment hearing if information not provided in time
  • Minister must designate a representative in certain circumstances
