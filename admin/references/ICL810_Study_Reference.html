Migration in Global Context

▼
"The overwhelming majority of people live in the country where they were born."
Key Statistics
  • International migration is NOT uniform across the world
  • Global displacement at record high: 1 in every 78 people on earth
  • Only 3.60% of world's population living outside country of birth
  • In 2017: ~15% answered "yes" to wanting to leave home country permanently
  • BUT fewer than 0.5% were actually preparing to migrate
  • The 13.5% gap (>600 million people) = legal barriers constructed through immigration law & policies
💡 Sources: IOM World Migration Report 2022 and UNHCR Global Trends

Canada as a Settler State — Historical Context

▼
Pre-Confederation Goals
Primary goal of Canadian immigration policy was to divest Indigenous population of "wild lands" and render them productive. Objective of building a white Canada translated to: (1) dispossession of Indigenous peoples from lands + (2) explicitly racist immigration policy post-Confederation.
Exclusionary Immigration Laws
  • First iteration of Indian Act passed 1876 — pass & permit systems
  • Immigration laws utilized to encourage "desirable" immigrants (hegemonic white "race") and exclude "undesirable" ones
  • Admission restrictions based on "race": People of African, Chinese, Japanese, Indian, Jewish, Caribbean ancestry subjected to discriminatory treatment
Colonial Citizenship
Immigration Act barred married women, minors, lunatics, and "idiots under a disability" from naturalization. "Canadians" were British subjects until 1947 when Canada enacted first Citizenship Act and Canadians became citizens of own country. New Act was silent on persons registered as "Indians" under Indian Act — despite being born in Canada, NOT Canadian citizens.
📜

Turning Points: Immigration Act 1976 & IRPA 2002

▼
Immigration Act, 1976 — A Turning Point
  • Explicit objectives articulated in s.3
  • Committed to non-discrimination
  • Shift from overt racism and exclusionary policies to systemic racism
IRPA 2002 — Current Framework
s.3(3) objectives include:
  • (d) Ensures decisions consistent with Canadian Charter including principles of equality and freedom from discrimination
  • (f) Complies with international human rights instruments to which Canada is signatory

Humanitarian & Compassionate (H&C) Applications

▼
IRPA s.25(1) allows Minister to grant permanent residence or exempt from IRPA requirements on humanitarian and compassionate grounds.
Key Characteristics
  • Available for applications outside OR inside Canada
  • Discretionary process — decision by immigration officer at first instance
  • Based on written application and submissions
  • Onus on applicant to demonstrate H&C grounds
  • Subject to exceptions under IRPA s.25(1.2)
💡 H&C grounds are NOT defined in legislation — developed through case law (see Baker and Kanthasamy).

FILAC Framework — Legal Reasoning

▼
FILAC — A Guide for Legal Reasoning (Thinking)
  • F — Facts (legally relevant facts)
  • I — Issues (legal questions to be answered)
  • L — Law (statutes, regulations, case law, policy)
  • A — Application (apply law to facts)
  • C — Conclusion (answer to legal issues)
💡 FILAC is for thinking through legal problems. CREAC (covered in Module 6) is for writing persuasive arguments.

Keyword Extraction Strategy

▼
Extract keywords in three tiers based on specificity:
1Y Keywords (Broad)
  • General legal area
  • Example: "immigration," "H&C"
2Y Keywords (Medium)
  • Specific legal concepts
  • Example: "establishment," "best interests of child"
3Y Keywords (Specific)
  • Unique fact patterns
  • Example: "single mother," "long-term residence"
Sources for Keywords
  • Client's facts
  • Legislation (IRPA, IRPR)
  • Case law headnotes
  • Secondary sources (textbooks, encyclopedias)
  • Policy documents (IRCC operational instructions)

SQ4R Approach to Reading & Briefing Case Law

▼
SQ4R for Active Reading of Cases
  • Survey — Why am I reading this case? What's the point?
  • Question — Skim to see structure. How long? Headings? Where are key parts?
  • Read — Mark parts of case & elements for brief
  • Recite — Describe court's reasoning in own words
  • Reflect — Relate case to larger area of law ("Observations")
  • Review — Identify relationship to other cases (pay attention to dates)
Case Brief Elements
  • Case name & citation
  • Procedural history
  • Facts
  • Issues
  • Decision (holding)
  • Reasoning (ratio decidendi)
  • Observations (significance, relationship to other cases)

Leading Cases: Baker & Kanthasamy

▼
Baker v Canada (Minister of Citizenship and Immigration), 1999 CanLII 699 (SCC), [1999] 2 SCR 817
Landmark case establishing procedural fairness requirements for H&C applications and factors for assessing best interests of the child.
Kanthasamy v Canada (Citizenship and Immigration), 2015 SCC 61, [2015] 3 SCR 909
Clarified that H&C relief should not be limited to "unusual and undeserved or disproportionate hardship" — a more flexible, compassionate approach required.

Administrative Law & Procedural Fairness

Why You Need to Know Administrative Law

▼
Immigration decisions are made by administrative tribunals and officers, not courts. Understanding administrative law principles is essential for:
  • Determining what procedural fairness is owed to clients
  • Identifying grounds for judicial review (bias, unreasonableness, procedural unfairness)
  • Understanding standard of review (correctness vs reasonableness)
  • Recognizing when decision-maker exceeded jurisdiction
📋

Baker v Canada — Five Factors for Procedural Fairness

▼
Baker v Canada (Minister of Citizenship and Immigration), [1999] 2 SCR 817
Established framework for determining what procedural fairness is required in administrative decisions.
The Five Baker Factors
1. Nature of the Decision
  • More important to individual = more fairness required
  • H&C decisions highly significant = higher fairness
2. Nature of the Statutory Scheme
  • Does legislation provide appeal? If yes, less fairness at first instance
  • H&C has no appeal = more fairness required at decision stage
3. Importance of Decision to Individual
  • Affects fundamental interests (family unity, safety) = more fairness
  • Baker: separation from children = highly important
4. Legitimate Expectations
  • Did practices/representations create expectation of certain procedure?
  • Procedural (not substantive) expectations protected
5. Choices of Procedures
  • What procedures has agency chosen? Are they appropriate?
  • Oral hearing not always required if written submissions sufficient
✅ Application: All 5 factors considered together — no single factor determinative. More procedural protections required when factors weigh in favor of individual.
⚖️

Impartiality, Bias & Independence

▼
Reasonable Apprehension of Bias Test
"...what would an informed person, viewing the matter realistically and practically—and having thought the matter through—conclude..."— Justice de Grandpré in Committee for Justice and Liberty v National Energy Board, [1978] 1 SCR 369
⚠️ Test is objective — not whether decision-maker was actually biased, but whether a reasonable, informed person would perceive bias.
Types of Bias
Actual Bias
  • Decision-maker has real prejudice or interest in outcome
  • Rarely proven
Institutional Bias
  • Structure of tribunal creates appearance of bias
  • Example: investigator also adjudicates
Reasonable Apprehension
  • Most common ground
  • Appearance of bias to reasonable observer
⚖️

Judicial Review vs Appeal

▼
Judicial Review
  • Narrower scope than full appeal
  • Reviews legality of decision (not merits)
  • Ensures decisions within authority, fair, correct or reasonable
  • Available in Federal Court for IRPA decisions
Appeals (IAD, RAD)
  • Full review of merits
  • Reviews decision-making process AND substance
  • Can substitute own decision
  • Only available where IRPA provides appeal right

Noting Up Legislation — Ensuring Currency

▼
Two-Part Process for Noting Up Legislation
1. Currency (In Force?)
  • Use Justice Laws (official source)
  • Plus LEGISinfo to backfill/enhance currency date
  • Provides official version with enhanced currency
2. Judicial Treatment
  • Use citator tool (CanLII, Westlaw KeyCite, Lexis QuickCITE)
  • How have courts interpreted/applied this provision?
  • Filter by jurisdiction, date, relevance

Entry to Canada — Who Needs What?

▼
Canadian Citizens
  • Valid passport OR
  • Enhanced driver's license (land/sea from US)
  • Right to enter — cannot be denied
Indigenous Peoples (Registered)
  • "Indian Status" card OR
  • Valid passport
  • Right to enter under Indian Act + IRPA s.19(1)
Permanent Residents
  • Valid PR card OR
  • Permanent Resident Travel Document (if card expired/lost)
  • Can be found inadmissible
Foreign Nationals
Generally require: (1) Temporary Resident Visa (TRV) if from visa-required country, (2) eTA if visa-exempt and arriving by air, (3) Valid passport. May also need work permit, study permit, etc.

Misrepresentation — IRPA s.40(1)(a)

▼
IRPA s.40(1)(a) — PR or FN inadmissible for directly or indirectly misrepresenting or withholding material facts relating to relevant matter that induces or could induce error in administration of Act.
Key Elements
  • Direct OR indirect misrepresentation
  • Withholding material facts also counts
  • Must be material — could affect decision
  • Induces or could induce error (doesn't need to actually induce)
  • No intent required — innocent mistake still misrepresentation
Case Studies — Is It Misrepresentation?
Wanda — Uncle Bob's Exaggeration
Application for PR (skilled worker). Uncle Bob exaggerates Wanda's education (claims Harvard M.A. instead of state university M.A.). Wanda doesn't notice.

YES — Misrepresentation — Indirect misrepresentation through representative. No intent required. Material fact (education) could induce error.
Charlene — Forgot One Husband
Married 5 times. Sponsored by Canadian husband Dan. Forgets one of her previous husbands on application.

YES — Misrepresentation — Withholding material fact. Marital history relevant to family class sponsorship.

Client-Centered Interviewing

▼
Client-centered interviewing focuses on client's goals, needs, and perspective rather than just gathering legal facts.
Key Principles
  • Active listening — Let client tell their story
  • Open-ended questions — "Tell me about..." vs "Did you..."
  • Empathy — Acknowledge emotions and concerns
  • Cultural competence — Understand client's cultural context
  • Clarification — Ensure you understand before giving advice
  • Collaboration — Client is partner in decision-making

Family Class — Who Can Sponsor & Be Sponsored?

▼
Who Can Be a Sponsor?
  • Canadian citizen or PR ≥18 years old
  • If citizen: can be outside Canada (must intend to return)
  • If PR: must be residing in Canada
  • Not receiving social assistance (except disability)
  • Meet income requirements (for parents/grandparents: LICO + 30%)
  • No criminal prohibitions (see ICL 850)
Who Can Be Sponsored?
Spouse (married/common-law/conjugal) Dependent child Parents & Grandparents Orphaned sibling/niece/nephew/grandchild

CREAC Framework — Legal Writing Structure

▼
CREAC — Persuasive Legal Writing
  • Conclusion — State your answer first (point-first writing)
  • Rule — State the relevant law (legislation, case law, policy)
  • Explanation — Explain how law is interpreted/applied
  • Application — Apply law to your client's specific facts
  • Conclusion — Restate conclusion with transitional phrase
Example: Marsela Case (Family Class Sponsorship)
C: Marsela's marriage to Edon is genuine for purposes of family class sponsorship.

R: IRPR s.4(1) states a FN is not member of family class if marriage "is not genuine."

E: Courts assess genuineness using M v H factors + ongoing nature of relationship.

A: Marsela and Edon have been in relationship for 3 years, have child together, communicate daily, share finances.

C: Therefore, Marsela's application should be approved as marriage is genuine.

Convention Refugee Definition — IRPA s.96

▼
IRPA s.96 — Convention refugee means person who, by reason of well-founded fear of persecution for reasons of race, religion, nationality, membership in particular social group, or political opinion:
  • (a) is outside each of their countries of nationality and unable or unwilling to avail themself of protection of each of those countries; OR
  • (b) not having a country of nationality, is outside country of former habitual residence and unable or unwilling to return to that country.
Five Elements to Establish
Inclusion Clause — Article 1A(2) / IRPA s.96
  • 1. Outside Country — Alienage requirement
  • 2. Well-Founded Fear — Subjective + objective components
  • 3. Persecution — Serious harm, sustained/systemic violation of rights
  • 4. Nexus — Connected to one of 5 grounds (race, religion, nationality, PSG, political opinion)
  • 5. Unable/Unwilling to Seek State Protection — State unable or unwilling to protect
⚖️

Well-Founded Fear & Persecution

▼
Well-Founded Fear (Two Components)
Subjective Fear
  • Claimant genuinely fears persecution
  • Assessed through testimony, demeanor, actions
Objective Basis
  • Reasonable person in claimant's circumstances would fear
  • Supported by country condition evidence
What Constitutes Persecution?
Persecution = serious harm + sustained or systemic violation of fundamental human rights demonstrative of failure of state protection.
  • Threat to life or freedom
  • Torture, cruel/unusual treatment or punishment
  • Sustained discrimination or harassment
  • Cumulative effect of measures violating rights
🔗

Nexus — Five Convention Grounds

▼
Race Religion Nationality Membership in Particular Social Group (PSG) Political Opinion
Particular Social Group (PSG) — Most flexible ground. Requirements: (1) Innate/unchangeable characteristic OR fundamental to identity/conscience, (2) Social visibility or "at risk" test, (3) State unable/unwilling to protect.

Examples of PSGs: Women fleeing gender-based violence, LGBTQ+ persons, family members of dissidents, former gang members who refuse to rejoin.

🚫

Exclusion & Cessation Clauses

▼
Exclusion Clauses (Article 1F) — NOT Entitled to Protection
  • 1F(a): Committed crime against peace, war crime, or crime against humanity
  • 1F(b): Committed serious non-political crime outside country of refuge before admission
  • 1F(c): Guilty of acts contrary to purposes and principles of United Nations
Cessation Clauses (Article 1C / IRPA s.108) — Protection Ends
  • Voluntarily re-availed of protection of country of nationality
  • Voluntarily re-acquired lost nationality
  • Acquired new nationality and enjoys protection of new country
  • Voluntarily re-established in country of persecution
  • Circumstances that led to recognition have ceased to exist
🇺🇸

Safe Third Country Agreement (STCA)

▼
Signed in 2004 between US and Canada. Refugee claimants arriving at land Port of Entry from US are ineligible to make claims in Canada.
Key Developments
  • Challenged under Charter s.7 due to poor US detention conditions
  • SCC held STCA does NOT violate Charter (upheld)
  • Referred back to FC for review of whether violates s.15 (equality) — esp. re: US treatment of gender-based violence claims
Exceptions
H&C considerations Policy exemptions (unaccompanied minors, family members in Canada) Administrative deferral of removals
💬

Trauma-Informed Interviewing

▼
Refugee claimants often experienced trauma. Interviews must be conducted with sensitivity to trauma's effects on memory, disclosure, and trust.
Key Principles
  • Safety first — Create safe, comfortable environment
  • Trustworthiness — Be transparent about process, confidentiality
  • Peer support — Consider interpreter from same community (with caution)
  • Collaboration — Empower claimant in process
  • Cultural sensitivity — Understand cultural norms around disclosure
  • Patience — Allow time, don't rush sensitive topics
  • Understand trauma effects — Inconsistencies may result from trauma, not dishonesty

FILAC vs CREAC — Complete Framework

▼
FILAC (Thinking)
  • F — Facts
  • I — Issues
  • L — Law
  • A — Application
  • C — Conclusion
CREAC (Writing)
  • C — Conclusion (answer first)
  • R — Rule (state the law)
  • E — Explanation (how law applies)
  • A — Application (to facts)
  • C — Conclusion (restate)
💡 Key Difference: FILAC is your analytical framework for thinking through legal problems. CREAC is your writing structure for persuasive legal arguments. CREAC uses "point-first" writing — state conclusion first, then prove it.
