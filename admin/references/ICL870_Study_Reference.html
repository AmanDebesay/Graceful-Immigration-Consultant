Module 1 — Part 1: Inadmissibility

Overview & Legal Framework of Inadmissibility

IRPA grounds: medical, financial, misrepresentation, cessation, non-compliance, inadmissible family
🏥

Medical Inadmissibility — IRPA s.38

▼
IRPA 38(1) — A foreign national is inadmissible on health grounds if their health condition:
(a) Likely danger to public health (b) Likely danger to public safety (c) Might cause excessive demand on health/social services
Standard of Proof
  • Balance of probabilities
Likely Outcome
  • Exclusion order — 1 year (though inadmissible for duration of medical condition)
Exceptions to s.38(1)(c) Excessive Demand
Refugee/protected person OR their immediate family member Being sponsored as member of family class (spouse or child)
💰

Financial Inadmissibility — IRPA s.39

▼
IRPA 39 — A foreign national is inadmissible for financial reasons if they are or will be unable or unwilling to support themselves or any dependent person, and have NOT satisfied an officer that adequate arrangements (other than social assistance) have been made.
Standard of Proof
  • Balance of probabilities
Likely Outcome
  • Exclusion order — 1 year
⚠️

Misrepresentation — IRPA s.40 (Most Common)

▼
IRPA 40(1)(a) — A permanent resident or foreign national is inadmissible for misrepresentation for directly or indirectly misrepresenting or withholding material facts relating to a relevant matter that induces or could induce an error in the administration of this Act.
Also Inadmissible If:
  • Sponsored by a person who is inadmissible for misrepresentation
  • Had refugee decision vacated
  • Had citizenship revoked for false representation, fraud, or knowingly concealing material circumstances
Standard of Proof
  • Balance of probabilities
Likely Outcome
  • 5-year exclusion order
Innocent Mistake Defence — Extremely Narrow!
Quintessential Example: A person raising a child they believed was theirs, but DNA test during processing reveals the child is not actually theirs — this would likely qualify for innocent mistake defence.
⚠️ Does NOT Qualify for Innocent Mistake:
  • Unaware of fraudulent actions by your representative
  • Failed to include employment because you didn't think it counted
  • Didn't disclose previous refusal assuming IRCC already has the info
  • Accidentally checked wrong box on form
🛑

Cessation of Refugee Protection — IRPA s.40.1

▼
s.40.1(1) — Foreign national inadmissible on final determination under s.108(2) that refugee protection has ceased.
s.40.1(2) — Permanent resident inadmissible on final determination that refugee protection ceased for reasons in s.108(1)(a)–(d).
📋

Non-Compliance with the Act — IRPA s.41

▼
IRPA 41 — A person is inadmissible for failing to comply with this Act:
(a) Foreign National
  • Through act or omission which contravenes (directly or indirectly) a provision of this Act
(b) Permanent Resident
  • Failing to comply with s.27(2) conditions of PR OR s.28 residency obligation
Likely Outcome
  • FN: Exclusion order
  • PR: Departure order
Standard of Proof
  • Balance of probabilities
👨‍👩‍👧

Inadmissible Family Member — IRPA s.42

▼
IRPA 42(1) — A foreign national (other than protected person) is inadmissible on grounds of an inadmissible family member if:
(a) Accompanying family member OR (in prescribed circumstances) non-accompanying family member is inadmissible (b) They are an accompanying family member of an inadmissible person
💡 Does NOT apply to temporary resident applications unless family member is inadmissible pursuant to IRPA ss.34, 35, 35.1, or 37 (security/human rights violations/organized crime).
Module 2 — Part 1: Inadmissibility

Immigration Division Rules & Procedure

ID jurisdiction, admissibility hearings, procedural rules, responding to PFLs
🏛️

Overview of the Immigration Division (ID)

▼
The Immigration Division is responsible for conducting admissibility hearings to determine if a foreign national or permanent resident is inadmissible to Canada under IRPA.
Key Functions
  • Conduct admissibility hearings for inadmissibility allegations
  • Conduct detention reviews to determine if continued detention is warranted
  • Issue removal orders if person found inadmissible
  • Make decisions on release conditions and alternatives to detention
📜

Admissibility Hearings

▼
Admissibility hearings are held when CBSA alleges that a person is inadmissible to Canada. The burden is on the Minister (CBSA) to prove inadmissibility on a balance of probabilities.
Process
1. Report of Inadmissibility
(CBSA issues s.44 report) → 2. Referral to ID
(Minister's delegate refers) → 3. Admissibility Hearing
(ID Member presides) → 4. Decision
(Inadmissible or not) → 5. Removal Order
(If inadmissible)
⚖️

Rules and Procedures of the ID

▼
The ID operates under the Immigration Division Rules (SOR/2002-229). Key procedural requirements:
Notice & Disclosure
  • Minister must provide notice of hearing
  • Minister must disclose evidence in advance
  • Person concerned has right to disclosure
  • Documents must be filed according to timelines
Hearing Conduct
  • Right to counsel
  • Right to interpreter
  • Right to call witnesses
  • Right to make submissions
📬

Critically Assessing Responses to PFLs

▼
Procedural Fairness Letters (PFLs) give the person an opportunity to respond to concerns BEFORE a negative decision is made. Effective responses are critical.
Steps to Respond to a PFL
  • Carefully read and identify ALL concerns raised
  • Research the law and policy applicable to each concern
  • Gather responsive documents and evidence
  • Draft a detailed response addressing each concern with legal references
  • Submit before the deadline (request extension if needed)
  • Follow up to confirm receipt
💡 A PFL response should be substantive, well-organized, and legally grounded. Generic or incomplete responses may lead to refusal.
Module 3 — Part 1: Inadmissibility

Immigration Appeal Division (IAD)

Jurisdiction, right of appeal, Ribic factors, evidence, orders, effective advocacy
⚖️

IAD Jurisdiction — IRPA s.62

▼
IRPA 62 — The Immigration Appeal Division is the competent Division of the Board with respect to appeals under this Division.
IAD Hears Appeals From:
Permanent Residents — removal orders (with exceptions) Foreign Nationals — sponsorship refusals Sponsors — sponsorship application refusals
📋

Right of Appeal — IRPA s.63

▼
Who has a right of appeal to the IAD?
Permanent Residents
  • May appeal removal order made at admissibility hearing
  • EXCEPT: If inadmissible on grounds of serious criminality and sentenced to ≥6 months OR ≥2 years imprisonment
  • EXCEPT: If inadmissible on security, human rights violations, or organized crime grounds
Foreign Nationals (Sponsors)
  • Sponsor may appeal refusal of family class sponsorship application
  • Sponsored FN may appeal refusal (if sponsor is citizen or PR)
⚠️ IRPA s.64 "Serious Criminality" for Appeal Rights: No appeal if convicted in Canada of offence punishable by max of ≥10 years OR sentenced to ≥6 months. Note: This is DIFFERENT from s.36 serious criminality for inadmissibility purposes.
📊

Ribic Factors — H&C Relief at IAD

▼
In determining whether to grant a stay of removal, the IAD considers humanitarian and compassionate (H&C) factors, as set out in the Ribic decision.
The Ribic Factors
  • Seriousness of the offence(s) leading to the removal order
  • Possibility of rehabilitation (or rehabilitation already achieved)
  • Length of time spent in Canada and degree of establishment
  • Family in Canada and dislocation to family that removal would cause
  • Support available from family and community
  • Degree of hardship that would be caused by removal
  • Any other relevant factors (best interests of children, country conditions, etc.)
✅ IAD has broad discretion to weigh these factors. Strong evidence on multiple factors increases likelihood of success.
📄

Evidence at IAD

▼
Evidence = proof; means of establishing facts. IAD proceedings are less formal than court — broad rules of evidence apply.
Types of Evidence
Documentary Evidence
  • Letters of support
  • Employment records
  • Medical/psychological reports
  • Police records, rehabilitation certificates
  • Country condition reports
Oral Testimony
  • Appellant's testimony
  • Family members
  • Community members
  • Expert witnesses (psychologists, etc.)
💡 Evidence must be reliable and probative. Generic letters or boilerplate documents carry little weight.
⚖️

IAD Orders / Decisions

▼
Possible IAD Outcomes
OrderDescriptionEffect
Allow AppealIAD finds removal order was not valid or is not justified on H&C groundsRemoval order set aside; person remains in Canada
Dismiss AppealIAD upholds removal order; no H&C relief warrantedRemoval order stands; person subject to removal
Stay of RemovalIAD grants conditional stay (typically 1–3 years) with termsRemoval order remains but enforcement is stayed; if conditions met, may appeal again later
✅ A stay is the most common outcome when H&C factors are strong but the removal order was validly issued. Conditions may include: reporting, no criminal activity, community service, counseling, etc.
🎤

Effective Written & Oral Advocacy

▼
Written Submissions (Pre-Hearing)
  • Clearly identify the issues and legal framework
  • Address each Ribic factor with specific evidence
  • Anticipate and respond to Minister's arguments
  • Cite relevant case law and policy
  • Be concise but comprehensive
Oral Submissions (At Hearing)
  • Prepare an outline, not a script
  • Lead with strongest arguments
  • Be responsive to Member's questions
  • Use respectful, professional tone
  • Emphasize rehabilitation and family ties
  • Acknowledge weaknesses but contextualize them
Module 4 — Part 1: Inadmissibility

Overcoming Inadmissibility

Criminal rehabilitation, H&C applications, TRPs, Ministerial Relief, ARC
🛡️

Criminal Inadmissibility — IRPA s.36

▼
When is a person inadmissible on criminality for convictions?
Committed offence equivalent to an offence punishable in Canada by a maximum of at least 10 years.
Key Points
  • Must be a conviction
  • Must be under an Act of Parliament (federal statute)
  • Hybrid offence = treated as indictable
  • Do NOT confuse s.36 serious criminality (inadmissibility) with s.64 serious criminality (appeal rights)
  • Look at the statute under which person was convicted to determine max punishment: ≥10 years triggers s.36(1)
✅

Criminal Rehabilitation

▼
Criminal rehabilitation removes the grounds of inadmissibility for past criminal conduct. It is a permanent solution.
Eligibility
TypeWaiting PeriodRequirements
Deemed Rehabilitation10 years since completion of sentenceSingle non-serious offence (max <10 yrs); no application needed, automatic
Individual Rehabilitation5 years since completion of sentenceSerious criminality OR multiple offences; must apply with evidence of rehabilitation
Rehabilitation Criteria (Officer Assessment)
  • Nature and severity of the offence(s)
  • Circumstances surrounding the offence
  • Period of time since the offence
  • Evidence of rehabilitation (employment, community ties, no reoffending)
  • Need to enter Canada
💡 Strong evidence package is essential: reference letters, employment records, police clearances, proof of stability.
📄

Humanitarian & Compassionate (H&C) Applications

▼
IRPA s.25(1) — Minister may grant PR status or exempt from IRPA requirements if justified by H&C considerations, taking into account best interests of any child directly affected.
H&C Factors (Non-Exhaustive)
  • Establishment in Canada (length of time, employment, community ties)
  • Best interests of any children affected
  • Hardship if required to apply from outside Canada
  • Family ties in Canada
  • Country conditions in country of origin
  • Health considerations
  • Any other relevant humanitarian factors
⚠️ H&C applications are discretionary and highly individualized. Officers have broad discretion. Strong documentary evidence is crucial.
🎫

Temporary Resident Permits (TRPs)

▼
IRPA s.24(1) — An officer may issue a TRP to a foreign national who is inadmissible or does not meet IRPA requirements, if the officer is of the opinion that it is justified in the circumstances.
Key Features
Purpose
  • Temporary solution (not permanent)
  • Allows entry despite inadmissibility
  • Discretionary — must justify need to enter
Duration & Conditions
  • Valid for specific period (officer decides)
  • May have conditions attached
  • Can be renewed if circumstances warrant
💡 TRP is often used for urgent/compassionate travel (e.g., attending funeral, critical business meeting) when criminal rehabilitation is not yet available.
🔄

Authorization to Return to Canada (ARC)

▼
If a person has been removed from Canada, they need an Authorization to Return to Canada (ARC) before they can return.
When Required
  • Person was previously removed from Canada
  • Person has a removal order in effect
  • Person was deported
Application Process
  • Must apply before attempting to return
  • Officer assesses reasons for previous removal and reasons to return
  • May be granted with conditions
Module 5 — Part 2: Detention

Legal Framework for Immigration Detention

IRPA s.58 grounds (danger, flight risk, ministerial inquiry, identity) · IRPR s.248 factors
🔒

Immigration Detention — Definition & Principles

▼
"Immigration detention is the practice of holding people who are subject to immigration control in custody, while they wait for permission to enter or before they are deported or removed from the country. It is an administrative process, not a criminal procedure."
⚠️ Detention as a Last Resort — Detention should only be used when there are no reasonable alternatives to detention available. Officers and ID members must consider alternatives first.
⏱️

Detention Review Timelines — IRPA s.57

▼
ReviewTimingFrequency
First ReviewWithin 48 hours of detentionOnce
Second ReviewWithin 7 days after first reviewOnce
Subsequent ReviewsAt least once every 30 daysOngoing until release or removal
📋

Two-Part Analysis at Every Detention Review

▼
Step 1:
Has a ground for detention been made out?
(IRPA s.58) → Step 2:
Are the s.248 factors such that detention remains justified?
(IRPR s.248)
💡 Even if a ground for detention exists, the ID Member must still assess all s.248 factors to determine if detention remains proportionate and justified. This is where alternatives to detention are considered.
⚠️

IRPA s.58(1) — Four Grounds for Detention

▼
(a) Danger to the Public
Person is a danger to the public.
IRPR s.246 — Danger Factors
  • Association with criminal organization
  • Engagement in people smuggling or trafficking
  • Conviction in Canada for sexual offence, offence involving violence/weapons, or offence causing death/serious harm
  • Conviction outside Canada that, if committed in Canada, would constitute such offence
💡 Non-Listed Factors: Mental health issues, substance abuse, history of violence, compliance with conditions, support network, rehabilitation efforts.
(b) Unlikely to Appear
Person is unlikely to appear for examination, admissibility hearing, removal, or proceeding that could lead to removal order.
Factors Suggesting Flight Risk
  • Previous failure to appear
  • No ties to Canada (family, employment, property)
  • History of non-compliance with immigration authorities
  • Use of false identity/documents
  • Weak travel documents or no documents
Factors Suggesting LOW Flight Risk
  • Strong family ties in Canada
  • Employment/business in Canada
  • Property ownership
  • Compliance history
  • Valid travel documents
  • Bondsperson available
(c) Ministerial Inquiry (Inadmissibility on Security/Human Rights Grounds)
Minister is taking necessary steps to inquire into a reasonable suspicion that person is inadmissible on grounds of: security (s.34), violating human or international rights (s.35), or organized criminality (s.37).
(d) Identity Not Established
The Minister is satisfied that person's identity has not been established.
Identity Issues May Include
  • No identity documents provided
  • Documents appear fraudulent
  • Multiple identities used
  • Inconsistent biographical information
Ways to Establish Identity
  • Passport or national ID from country of origin
  • Birth certificate
  • Biometric verification
  • Corroborating documents (school records, etc.)
⚖️

IRPR s.248 — Factors for Detention/Release Assessment

▼
Even if a ground for detention is made out, the ID Member must consider each of the s.248 factors to determine if detention remains proportionate and justified.
The s.248 Factors
  • (a) Reason for detention and strength of the case
  • (b) Length of time in detention
  • (c) Existence of alternatives to detention
  • (d) Immigration history and compliance with previous orders
  • (e) Criminal record and nature of offences
  • (f) Pattern of criminal activity or convictions
  • (g) Danger to the public
  • (h) Health concerns, including mental health
  • (i) Ties to the community, including family, employment, financial resources
  • (j) Hardship that detention may cause to the person or their family
  • (k) Reasons person came to Canada
  • (l) Cooperation with CBSA
  • (m) Any other relevant factor
✅ Alternatives to detention under s.248(c) are critical. Propose specific, workable alternatives: reporting conditions, GPS monitoring, bondsperson, residence at specific address, surrender of travel documents, etc.
📖

Guidelines on Detention

▼
The Chairperson of the Immigration and Refugee Board has issued Guidelines on detention. While not legally binding, they are persuasive and should be referenced in submissions.
Key Guideline Principles
  • Detention should be a measure of last resort
  • Alternatives to detention must be considered
  • Vulnerable persons (children, pregnant women, elderly) require special consideration
  • Length of detention must be proportionate to the circumstances
  • Conditions in detention facilities must be humane
Module 6 — Part 2: Detention

Preparing for the Detention Review

Client interviews, bondsperson selection, alternatives to detention, disclosure, release plans
📝

Step 1: Investigate and Gather Information

▼
Establish Contact with the Client
  • Arrange in-person or telephone interview as soon as possible
  • Explain your role and the detention review process
  • Build trust and rapport
  • Assess client's mental and physical state
Information You Need from Your Client
  • Immigration history (how they came to Canada, previous applications, compliance)
  • Family ties in Canada (who, where, nature of relationship)
  • Employment history and current employment prospects
  • Community ties (religious community, organizations, friends)
  • Financial situation and assets
  • Criminal history (if any) and circumstances surrounding offences
  • Health concerns (physical and mental)
  • Understanding of the grounds for detention
  • Potential bondspersons
  • Plans if released (residence, employment, compliance)
📂

Step 2: Obtain Evidence About Previous DRs

▼
If retained after the 48-hour review, you need to understand what happened at previous detention reviews.
Documents to Obtain
Previous hearing transcripts Previous disclosure made by the Minister Previous disclosure provided by your client Previous ID Member's reasons for decision
💡 Review these carefully to understand: What grounds were raised? What evidence was presented? What were the Member's concerns? What alternatives were proposed (if any)? Why was detention continued?
🎯

Step 3: Develop Theory of Your Case & Gather Evidence

▼
Based on the ground(s) for detention, develop a strategy to address each ground and the s.248 factors.
Evidence to Rebut IRPA s.58 Grounds
GroundRebuttal Evidence
Danger to publicRehabilitation evidence, psychological assessment, letters of support, clean record since offence, addiction treatment completion, employment, family ties
Flight riskStrong ties to Canada (family, employment, property), compliance history, valid travel docs, bondsperson, proposed reporting conditions
IdentityPassport, birth certificate, ID from country of origin, corroborating documents, biometric verification
Evidence to Support s.248 Factors
  • Letters of support from family, employer, community members
  • Employment letter or job offer
  • Proof of residence (lease, utility bills)
  • Evidence of financial resources
  • Medical reports (if health issues)
  • Rehabilitation certificates or program completion
  • Bondsperson's affidavit and financial documents
📬

Step 4: Obtain Disclosure from the Minister

▼
Guideline 2 sets out the Minister's disclosure obligations in detention reviews.
What Must the Minister Disclose?
  • All evidence the Minister intends to rely on
  • CBSA case notes and reports
  • Criminal records and warrants
  • Immigration history and previous enforcement actions
  • Any evidence regarding the grounds for detention
⚠️ If disclosure is incomplete or late, raise a procedural fairness issue with the ID Member and request an adjournment if necessary.
👥

Selecting and Preparing a Bondsperson

▼
A good bondsperson is critical for release. They must be credible, financially stable, and willing to supervise the client.
Characteristics of a Good Bondsperson
  • Canadian citizen or permanent resident
  • Stable employment and income
  • Owns property or has significant savings
  • Has a genuine relationship with the client (family, close friend)
  • Understands their responsibilities
  • Willing and able to post a bond (cash deposit)
  • Lives in Canada (preferably nearby)
  • No criminal record
Preparing the Bondsperson for the Hearing
  • Explain the purpose of their testimony
  • Review their affidavit with them
  • Explain their obligations (report breaches, ensure compliance)
  • Prepare them for cross-examination by Minister's counsel
  • Ensure they bring proof of income and assets
📋

Developing a Comprehensive Release Plan

▼
A detailed, realistic release plan addresses the grounds for detention and demonstrates how risks will be managed.
Components of a Strong Release Plan
  • Residence: Specific address where client will reside (with bondsperson or family)
  • Reporting: Willingness to report to CBSA at specified intervals
  • Bondsperson: Identified person with financial capacity and relationship to client
  • Bond amount: Proposed cash deposit (consider bondsperson's financial means)
  • Surrender of documents: Passport and travel documents to be held by CBSA
  • No criminal activity: Commitment to obey all laws
  • Employment/education: Plans for employment or enrollment in programs
  • Support services: Counseling, addiction treatment, mental health support (if applicable)
  • GPS monitoring: If appropriate and available
  • No contact orders: If relevant to danger concerns
Module 7 — Part 2: Detention

Conducting a Detention Review

Witness prep, submissions best practices, hearing conduct, post-hearing remedies
👥

Preparing Witnesses/Bondspersons for DR

▼
Key Steps in Witness Preparation
  • Review their written statement or affidavit with them
  • Explain the format of the hearing and their role
  • Practice direct examination questions
  • Prepare them for possible cross-examination by Minister's counsel
  • Remind them to answer only the question asked
  • Advise them to be honest — if they don't know something, say so
  • Explain the importance of maintaining composure and professionalism
  • Ensure they bring all necessary documents (ID, financial documents)
💡 A well-prepared bondsperson who is clear, credible, and understands their responsibilities can make the difference between release and continued detention.
📝

Preparing Yourself for a DR

▼
Counsel's Pre-Hearing Checklist
  • Review all disclosure from the Minister
  • Review all previous DR transcripts and decisions
  • Organize your evidence and create an index
  • Prepare an outline of your submissions
  • Anticipate Minister's arguments and prepare responses
  • Know the law: relevant IRPA/IRPR sections, case law, Guidelines
  • Prepare questions for your client and bondsperson
  • Prepare cross-examination questions for Minister's witnesses (if any)
  • Have copies of all documents for Member and Minister's counsel
🎤

Preparing Submissions

▼
Effective submissions are clear, organized, and directly address the grounds for detention and all s.248 factors.
Structure of Submissions
1. Introduction
Overview of your position → 2. Address s.58 Ground
Why ground not made out OR mitigated → 3. Address s.248 Factors
Go through each factor → 4. Alternative to Detention
Present detailed release plan → 5. Conclusion
Request for release + conditions
✅

Best Practices for Submissions

▼
  • Be concise: Respect the Member's time; focus on strongest points
  • Be responsive: Address the specific concerns raised by the Member or Minister
  • Use evidence: Reference specific documents in your record
  • Cite law: Reference IRPA/IRPR sections, case law, Guidelines
  • Acknowledge weaknesses: Don't ignore problems — address them head-on and contextualize
  • Emphasize alternatives: Show that detention is not necessary — alternatives exist
  • Be professional: Maintain respectful tone, even if you disagree with Minister's position
  • Listen actively: Pay attention to Member's questions and concerns
⚖️

During the Hearing — Advocacy Tips

▼
  • Arrive early and ensure client and witnesses are ready
  • Introduce yourself and your client for the record
  • Make preliminary applications if needed (adjournment, disclosure issues)
  • Present your evidence methodically (client testimony, bondsperson, documents)
  • Object to improper questions during cross-examination (if necessary)
  • Deliver clear, organized submissions
  • Respond to Member's questions directly and honestly
  • Request specific terms and conditions for release
  • Thank the Member at the conclusion
📋

Post-Hearing Applications

▼
If detention is continued, there are post-hearing remedies available.
1. Application for Early Detention Review (EDR)
File if:
  • You didn't have an adequate release plan but now you do
  • You are newly retained and can address the grounds for detention
  • You have new evidence to rebut CBSA's allegations or ID's previous findings
2. Request a Change in Conditions
File if:
  • Client has been released on terms and conditions
  • A bondsperson is no longer willing or able to continue
  • Material change in client's circumstances (e.g., probation conditions changed)
  • Time has passed and risk has reduced → previous conditions no longer proportionate
How to Make an Application (ID Rules 37–40)
  • State explicitly what you are requesting
  • Set out why the request should be granted
  • Provide supporting evidence (e.g., new alternative to detention, evidence of compliance)
  • File with ID and serve on Minister's counsel

Pre-Removal Risk Assessment (PRRA) — IRPA s.112–114

▼
IRPA s.112 — A person in Canada (other than protected person) may apply for protection if subject to removal order that is in force.
Main Characteristics of PRRA
  • Assesses risk of persecution (s.96), risk to life/cruel treatment (s.97)
  • Focus is on forward-looking risk — what will happen if returned now
  • Generally based on written submissions and documents (no oral hearing unless credibility concerns)
  • Officer must consider personalized risk to the individual
  • Can include new evidence that arose after refugee claim or was not reasonably available at the time
  • If PRRA is positive → protected person status granted
  • If PRRA is negative → removal proceeds (can seek judicial review at FC)
💡 New Evidence: Must show evidence could not have been presented earlier with reasonable diligence OR is new evidence that arose after previous decision.
💚

H&C Application for Removal Cases

▼
H&C applications can be made from within Canada to avoid having to leave to apply for PR from abroad. Same factors as Module 4, but emphasis on:
  • Establishment in Canada
  • Best interests of children affected by removal
  • Hardship of having to apply from outside Canada
  • Country conditions in country of removal
⚠️ H&C processing can take many months or years. Does NOT automatically stay removal unless a deferral is granted or stay is obtained from Federal Court.
⏸️

Deferrals of Removal

▼
A deferral is a temporary postponement of removal. It is discretionary and granted by CBSA.
Grounds for Deferral
  • Pending PRRA or H&C application that has a reasonable chance of success
  • Medical emergency (person too ill to travel)
  • Humanitarian reasons (e.g., family emergency, wedding, funeral)
  • Awaiting judicial review decision (though JR does not automatically stay removal)
💡 Deferrals are temporary and must be requested from CBSA in writing with supporting evidence. CBSA has discretion to grant or deny.
⚠️

Ineffective Assistance of Counsel

▼
Allegations of ineffective assistance of counsel can form the basis for new evidence in a PRRA or for a motion to reopen proceedings.
Requirements to Establish Ineffective Assistance
  • Counsel's acts or omissions were outside the range of reasonable competence
  • There is a reasonable probability that the outcome would have been different but for counsel's errors
  • Applicant must provide affidavit evidence detailing specific failures
  • Former counsel must be given notice and opportunity to respond
⚠️ These claims are taken seriously and require substantial evidence. Not every mistake or disagreement with counsel's strategy qualifies.
⚖️

Magonza v. Canada — Balancing IRPA Goals

▼
Magonza v. Canada (Citizenship and Immigration), 2019 FC 14
This case illustrates the tension between two IRPA goals: (1) efficient processing and removal of inadmissible persons, and (2) procedural fairness and protection of refugee claimants.
Key Findings in Magonza
The court examined:
  • Whether the officer properly considered new evidence
  • Whether procedural fairness was afforded
  • The balance between finality of decisions and access to justice
💡 Takeaway: PRRA officers must genuinely consider new evidence and personalized risk. Boilerplate reasoning or failure to engage with evidence can be grounds for judicial review.
